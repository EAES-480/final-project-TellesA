Lead (Pb) Concentrations in Pilsen Surface Soils
by Arianna Telles

Summary
For my final project, I explored data that I collected for my independent research project this semester that determined lead (Pb) concentrations within the surface soils of the Pilsen neighborhood in Chicago. Many urban soils are often contaminated with lead from historical sources like leaded gasoline or leaded paint, as well as pollution from industrial sources. Pilsen is known for its industrial corridor that has contaminated the air and the soil within this neighborhood. Though most of the dangerous facilities have been shut down, there are still two potential polluters that are still running: Fisk, a coal-run powerplant, and SIMS, a metal recycling facility. 

I worked with the Pilsen Environmental Rights and Reform Organization (PERRO) to sample various sites where high community engagement and vulnerable populations (like children) overlap. This includes 2 parks (Harrison Park and Dvorak Park), 1 school (Irma Ruiz Elementary School, and El Paseo Community Garden. We also sampled along the industrial corridor where Fisk and SIMS are located, and within the neighborhood along a residential transect. To take these measurements I used a portable x-ray fluorescence spectrometer (pXRF) that can measure the concentrations of any present elements within a surface soil sample. My sampling design was site-specific based on the presence of publicly available soil. With this data, the research question I proposed is: How do lead levels with the surface soil vary across these different screening locations? To test this, I first created a boxplots and viewed the summary statistics to get a sense of their relationship and remove any outliers. Once I recognized a relationship, I ran an ANOVA between the lead concentrations and sample locations in order to identify the effects from these sites. After this I ran a linear regression between lead concentrations and sampling sites to determine which site may explain this variability and by how much. I also decided to group the sites into different categories to determine any relationship between the type of site. To do this, I grouped the sites into 5 categories: School, Park, Garden, Residential, and Industrial Corridor. With these groups, I ran a linear regression to determine if this categorization may improve my linear model. The results from the ANOVA show that there is a significant difference in the mean values between each sampling site (p-value < 0.001). Therefore, we have evidence to reject the null hypothesis that all group means are equal. For the first linear regression, all sites were compared to the Cermak2 transect, located within the industrial corridor. These results showed that the 2 parks, elementary school, and garden are significantly lower than Cermak2 (p-value < 0.001). Also, the two other portions of the industrial corridor, Fisk and SIMS are also significantly lower than Cermak2 but not as low as the community-based locations (p < 0.05). Lastly, the residential transect was found to have no significant difference. This linear regression had an R-squared of 0.1861, meaning that the sites explained about 18.61% of the variability within lead concentrations. The second linear regression showed a slightly higher R-squared of 0.1957, meaning this linear model explained more of the variability within lead concentrations. These results compared each of the site categories to the garden category. It showed that the industrial corridor category (p-value < 0.05) and residential transect (p-value < 0.001) are both significantly higher than the garden. Additionally, there was no significant difference between the garden, the park category, and the school. Based on these results I can conclude that the site locations of parks, schools, and gardens have significantly lower lead concentrations than the industrial corridor and residential area. This could be due to consistent maintenance of these locations. Furthermore, the residential area is significantly higher than the industrial corridor and community-based locations. Therefore, the type of the site does appear to play a role in how surface soil lead concentrations vary across the Pilsen neighborhood, although there may be different factors driving its variability such as distance from potential polluters or historical lead contamination.
